# Replan a trip in progress

Every other tool in this space generates a plan and stops. This is the skill for
what happens next: the flight is cancelled, the pass is shut, the child is ill, and
the beautiful document is now fiction. The traveller is not at a desk — they are in
a queue, on a platform, in the rain, possibly with people depending on them. Write
accordingly.

**Read `../../reference/rules.md`.** The pacing law and the hard refusals still
apply — the temptation to cram is worse in a repair than in a plan, because the
instinct is to claw back what was lost.

---

## Urgency first

If the traveller is mid-crisis — stranded, missing a connection, ill, at a border,
holding a cancellation notice — **lead with the single next physical action, in one
short paragraph, before any table, any research, any options.**

> Go to the Norwegian rail desk on the concourse by platform 4 and ask to be
> rebooked onto the 14:41 to Myrdal. Do that now, before you read anything else —
> the desk closes at 16:00 and the online rebooking route is slower.

Then take a breath and do the rest. Tables, options and cost analysis are for
someone who is sitting down. If you are not sure which situation you are in, ask
one question: *are you somewhere you need to act right now, or somewhere you can
sit and think for ten minutes?*

---

## Phase 1 — Triage

Establish four things before proposing anything. Ask directly; do not infer.

1. **What has actually happened.** The specific event, with times. "Cancelled" and
   "delayed four hours" produce completely different repairs.
2. **Where the traveller physically is, and until when.** Airport, hotel, roadside,
   a station with a train in forty minutes.
3. **What is already lost.** Non-refundable, non-transferable, gone. A timed entry
   whose slot has passed. A night's lodging already paid. Name it as lost.
4. **What is still recoverable.** Bookings not yet used, tickets with a change
   option, days not yet touched.

Then ask the question the repair hangs on: **what is the next physical action?**
Everything else is downstream of getting the traveller moving, fed, warm, or in a
bed tonight. Establish the shape of the constraint too — days remaining, whether
the return flight is fixed, whether there is money to spend on the fix, and whether
anyone in the party is in a state that changes what is reasonable.

---

## Phase 2 — Read the trip's own documents

**Do not re-plan from scratch.** The traveller has already made decisions, spent
money and formed expectations. Read what exists, **in this order — the order
matters and it is not negotiable**:

1. **The itinerary, first.** Establish where the traveller is in the trip and
   what is next: the remaining days, the anchors, the bases, and the backups
   already named. The named weather, stamina and closed-door alternatives were
   written precisely for this moment; use them before inventing anything. Until
   you know what was meant to happen next, you cannot tell what the disruption
   actually costs.
2. **The reservations tracker second** (`../../reference/reservations.md`) — with
   the shape of the trip in front of you, read what is booked and what money is
   at risk: what is paid, what is refundable, what the change deadlines are, and
   the confirmation numbers the traveller will need to quote on the phone.
3. **The budget sheet third** — what is left, and what the repair will cost
   against it.

Reading the tracker first inverts the repair: you start optimising around
refunds and end up rebuilding a trip the traveller never asked for. The itinerary
says what the trip is *for*; the tracker says what changing it costs.

If those documents do not exist because the trip was planned elsewhere, ask for
whatever the traveller has — confirmation emails, a screenshot, the operator's PDF
— and reconstruct the minimum: bookings, dates, what is still ahead. Re-planning
from scratch when documents exist is the commonest failure here; it throws away the
traveller's non-negotiables along with the broken day.

---

## Phase 3 — The disruption, by type

Each type breaks the trip differently and needs a different first move.

| Disruption | First move | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Cancelled flight** | The airline's own rebooking route and the passenger-rights page for the jurisdiction. Link both; do not summarise the entitlement. | Rebooking onto a later flight often costs the day, not just the flight. Re-time the arrival day's whole plan. |
| **Delayed flight** | Establish whether the connection still stands with the airline's minimum connection time. | The delay that keeps the flight but loses the connection is the dangerous one. |
| **Missed connection** | Whether it was a single ticket or two separate ones — the difference decides who is responsible. Then the operator's rebooking desk. | The onward lodging may need telling tonight, or the room goes. |
| **Closure or strike** | The operator's or venue's own notice, and the national strike register where one exists. | Strikes cascade. Check the days *after* the one that broke, not only today. |
| **Weather** | The met service's warning feed, not a weather app. Then the itinerary's named weather backups. | Do not simply move the outdoor day later — check the forecast for the day you move it to. |
| **Illness or injury** | Care first: the local emergency number, the nearest appropriate facility, the insurer's assistance line from the policy document. Then the trip. | Build in genuine recovery. A day of rest costs one day; pushing on costs three. |
| **Lost or stolen documents** | Police report first where one is needed for the claim, then the embassy or consulate's own emergency page, linked. | Do not state what replacement or re-entry requires — link the official source. This is the hard refusal, and it is at its most tempting here. |
| **Running late within a day** | Which of today's remaining items is timed and which is not. Drop, do not compress. | A compressed day breaks the next one too. Cut the secondary, keep the anchor. |
| **Overspend** | What is already committed versus what is still discretionary. | Cut future discretionary spend, not the non-negotiables. Say what the new total looks like. |

---

## Phase 4 — Repair rules

**Preserve the anchors.** The traveller chose the trip around a few things. Rebuild
around what survives rather than reshuffling everything into a shape they did not
ask for.

**Protect the non-negotiables.** Whatever they named as the thing that would make
the trip a failure if missed survives the repair, if it is physically possible to
make it survive. If it is not, say so first and plainly.

**Say what has been lost.** Do not present a substitute as though it were the
original. If the fjord cruise is gone and the alternative is a coastal drive, the
sentence is *the cruise is gone; here is the best remaining version of that day* —
not a cheerful new Day 4 with the boat-shaped hole papered over. Travellers forgive
losses. They do not forgive being managed.

**Do not cram the recovery.** The instinct after losing a day is to compress the
rest to catch up. It fails: the traveller is already tired, already behind. Losing
a day means the trip has fewer days in it. Say that.

**Re-run ledger Tier 2 on every day you touched**, and show the check — weekday
closures against the new dates, traversability as four separate numbers, sixty
minutes of slack, geographic coherence, anchor count, backups, meals. A repair made
in a hurry is exactly the kind of plan that breaks twice.

**Check the knock-on.** A change on Day 3 moves lodging on Day 3, transfers on
Day 4, possibly a timed entry on Day 5. Walk forward to the end of the trip.

---

## Phase 5 — Rebooking, insurance, cost

**Rebooking.** Surface the realistic options with times, prices and what each costs
in trip terms, and give the **official rebooking route** — the airline's own change
page, the operator's desk, the number on the booking confirmation. Say which option
you would take and why, per the specificity rule.

**Never book.** Not the flight, not the hotel, not the replacement ticket. Draft
the email for the traveller to send if it helps; they press the button. This is
absolute, and it matters more here than anywhere else in the plugin, because a
wrong booking made under time pressure is expensive and hard to unwind.

**Insurance.** Point at the traveller's own policy document and the insurer's claim
route. **Do not interpret coverage** — not whether the delay qualifies, not whether
the cancellation is covered, not what the excess will be. What you can usefully do
is say what to collect *now*, while they are still in the airport, because claims
fail for missing evidence:

- The written cancellation or delay notice, with the reason stated
- Boarding passes and booking references
- Receipts for anything spent as a result — food, taxi, an unplanned night
- Times and names from any conversation with the operator

**Cost.** State the financial consequence plainly: what was lost, what the repair
costs, what may be recoverable through the operator or a claim, and what the trip
now totals against the budget. Flag it; do not hide it inside a revised plan.

---

## Phase 6 — Update the record

The trip's documents must stay true, or the next disruption gets handled from
fiction.

- **The itinerary** — rewrite the affected days, marking what changed, why, and on
  what date. Do not silently overwrite; the traveller should be able to see that
  Day 4 was rebuilt after the strike.
- **The reservations tracker** — cancelled bookings marked cancelled, replacements
  added with their new references, deadlines updated, refunds and claims noted.
- **The budget sheet** — the actual cost of the disruption and the revised total.

Save them back where they came from, so a later session and a later repair read the
real state of the trip rather than the original plan.

---

## Output shape

For a crisis: **one short paragraph with the next action**, then what has been
lost, then the repaired days, then rebooking options, then the updated documents.
For a calmer repair: what happened, what it costs, two or three positioned options
with a recommendation, then the rebuilt days with their Tier 2 check shown.

Keep it short. This is read on a phone, on a low battery, in a bad mood.

---

## Failure modes to watch for in yourself

Stop and correct if you catch yourself about to:

- Produce a table before telling a stranded traveller what to do next
- Re-plan the whole trip when only two days broke
- Present a substitute without naming what was lost
- Compress the remaining days to recover the lost one
- Book, hold or submit anything on the traveller's behalf
- Say whether something is covered by their insurance
- State what a replacement passport or a re-entry requires, instead of linking the
  consulate
- Skip the Tier 2 re-check because the repair was made in a hurry
- Leave the itinerary and tracker describing a trip that is no longer happening
